---
title: 'Retention starts with a clear customer action'
date: '2026-11-12'
description: 'A practical framework for defining activation, reading cohorts, and choosing your next retention experiment.'
---

Before building a churn model, define what a customer needs to do to receive value. An app open, a purchase, and a completed workflow answer different questions. Counting all three as engagement hides the decision you need to make.

## Define the behaviour

Write down the customer, the action, and the expected frequency. A daily workflow product and a seasonal agricultural marketplace should not share a retention target. Choose a meaningful return event and a time window that fits the actual use case.

## Follow cohorts, not just totals

Group customers by when they first reached the activation event. Compare the proportion who return in later periods. Keep the cohort definition consistent and separate incomplete observation windows from genuine drop-off.

Then segment carefully. Acquisition channel, customer type, first use case, and onboarding path can explain why the overall average moved. Small cohorts need caution: a few customers can change the percentage substantially.

## Connect the chart to a hypothesis

A drop in the first week might point to an onboarding problem. Later drop-off could reflect repeated friction, a mismatch in expectations, pricing, or a use case that has ended. The chart identifies where to investigate; interviews and product evidence help explain why.

For each hypothesis, specify an intervention, a success measure, a guardrail, and when you will decide whether to continue. Test one clear change where possible. Track whether the change improves the behaviour that represents value, rather than only the number of messages sent or screens viewed.

## Make the analysis usable

A useful handover includes the event definition, the cohort query, the observation window, and the assumptions. It should also say what remains unknown and which experiment would reduce that uncertainty.
